## What Makes a Contractor Right for an Insurance Rebuild, Specifically?

An insurance-ready contractor writes estimates in the same software your adjuster uses, documents damage the way a claims file requires, and has done enough of these jobs to know what gets denied versus what gets paid. That's different from general remodeling experience.

Most remodeling contractors are excellent at kitchens and additions priced against a homeowner's budget. A claims rebuild is priced against a carrier's scope of loss, using line-item codes (Xactimate pricing) that both sides recognize. A contractor who doesn't write estimates that way will underbid the job, then have to come back asking for more money mid-project, or eat the difference and cut corners to make it work.

Ask directly: "Do you write your estimates in Xactimate, and can you show me a sample?" If the answer is a blank look, keep looking.

## Insurance Restoration Contractor vs. a Regular General Contractor: What's the Real Difference?

An insurance restoration contractor is built around the claims process from day one; a regular general contractor treats an insurance job as just another remodel with a different funding source. That distinction shows up in three places: documentation, code-upgrade knowledge, and who talks to the adjuster.

A restoration-focused contractor photographs and moisture-maps the damage before demo starts, because that documentation is what justifies the scope later. They also know when local or Texas building code requires an upgrade beyond simple like-for-like replacement (a GFCI circuit added where there wasn't one, for example) and can write that into a supplement instead of eating the cost. A generalist contractor often finds those requirements mid-job, after the budget is already set.

## Who Coordinates With the Insurance Adjuster on a Rebuild?

Whoever performed the mitigation should be the one talking to the adjuster during the rebuild, because they're the one holding the original moisture readings, photos, and category/class documentation the scope is based on. Handing that file to a separate rebuild contractor means re-explaining the loss, often from scratch.

In practice this means one point of contact walks the adjuster through the property, answers questions about what was removed and why, and submits any supplement request with the documentation attached, not a verbal estimate. ## What Belongs in an Itemized Estimate, and What's a Supplement?

An itemized estimate lists every material, labor hour, and line-item code tied to the approved scope, so you and the carrier can both see exactly what's being paid for and why. A supplement is a follow-up request for additional covered work discovered once demo opens the walls, like hidden water migration or code-required upgrades.

Before you sign a contract, ask for the itemized breakdown, not a one-page lump-sum quote. A lump sum hides whether the drywall, texture, paint, trim, and flooring are each priced to match what the adjuster approved, and it makes a legitimate supplement harder to prove later. Supplements are normal on insurance rebuilds, especially after a hail or pipe-burst loss where damage is often worse once materials come out. What's not normal is a contractor who treats every supplement as a surprise rather than documenting it against photos and moisture readings as the job progresses.

## Should Mitigation and the Rebuild Be One Company, or Two?

One company, when that company is actually licensed and staffed for both phases, almost always produces a cleaner claim and a faster timeline than splitting mitigation and reconstruction between two firms. The handoff between separate companies is where documentation gets lost and scopes get re-argued.

When mitigation and rebuild sit with different companies, the rebuild contractor often has to re-verify drying logs, moisture readings, and the original category of the water loss before they'll warranty their own work over it, which the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) requires drying be verified against in the first place. That re-verification costs time and sometimes a second inspection fee. ## What to Ask Before You Sign a Rebuild Contract in Texas

Before signing, confirm the contractor's license is active with the [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/LicenseSearch/), get the itemized estimate in writing, and get a straight answer on who files the supplement if one comes up.

A short list worth going through line by line:

- Is your license number current, and can I verify it myself?
- Will you write the estimate in Xactimate or a comparable line-item format?
- Who talks to my adjuster if the scope needs to change mid-job?
- Do you carry the IICRC credentials for the trade involved (water, fire, mold), or is that a subcontractor?
- What's the payment schedule, and does it track with insurance disbursement draws rather than requiring full payment up front?

If a contractor can't answer two or more of those cleanly, that's reason enough to call someone else.
