## Is it really a slab leak, or something else?

The combination of a rising water bill, a warm or damp spot on the floor, and the sound of running water when every fixture in the house is off points to a slab leak more reliably than any single sign alone.

Other clues: a hot spot under tile or laminate (usually means the hot water line is the one leaking), a musty smell near a baseboard with no visible source, or cracking in the slab or nearby drywall that wasn't there before. A warm spot alone could also be a heating duct, so combine it with the water bill before assuming the worst.

If the leak is active and visible, like water pooling around a fixture or coming up through a crack, shut off the home's main water valve immediately. That stops the loss from growing while you make calls.

## Who do you call first: plumber, leak detection, or restoration company?

Call a leak detection service or licensed plumber first to pinpoint the exact location of the leak before anyone cuts into the slab; call the restoration side once water has already damaged flooring, baseboards, or drywall.

Electronic leak detection uses acoustic listening equipment and sometimes infrared imaging to locate the leak within a foot or two, without guessing and jackhammering the whole slab. That detection work and the actual pipe repair, whether it's a spot repair, a full reroute overhead through the attic, or a reline, has to be done by a licensed plumber. Reign Restoration does not hold a plumbing license and does not repair the pipe itself, so be upfront about that when you call: the company handles locating moisture, drying the structure, and rebuilding what the leak damaged, and a plumber handles the line.

Most homeowners end up coordinating both trades at once. A practical order looks like this:

1. Shut off the main water valve if the leak is active.
2. Call a restoration company for moisture assessment and leak detection coordination, especially if flooring or walls are already wet.
3. Get a licensed plumber scheduled to locate and repair the actual line.
4. Once the leak is stopped, the restoration side dries the slab and framing, removes wet flooring, baseboards, or drywall that can't be saved, and rebuilds.

## What does a slab leak do to the flooring and framing above it?

A slab leak traps moisture under flooring and inside wall cavities for days or weeks before anyone notices, which means tile, laminate, hardwood, and the baseboards and drywall nearest the floor are usually wet well beyond the spot you can see.

Hardwood and laminate over a slab leak almost always need to come up once the subfloor underneath has absorbed water; tile sometimes can be dried in place depending on the setting bed. Drywall and baseboards wick water upward from the slab, so a 2-foot flood cut (removing drywall up to roughly two feet above the visible waterline) is common even when the leak itself only affected a small area. Left untreated, trapped moisture under a slab can support mold growth within 24 to 48 hours ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home)).

If you're not sure whether your flooring is salvageable, the comparison in [Hardwood Floor Water Damage](/blog/hardwood-floor-water-damage/) walks through what typically gets dried versus removed.

## Will homeowners insurance cover a slab leak in Texas?

Most Texas homeowners policies cover the sudden, accidental water damage caused by a slab leak, like the cost to dry and rebuild the flooring and walls, but they typically exclude the cost of locating and repairing the pipe itself unless your policy has a specific water backup or service line endorsement.

The distinction insurers draw is between the damage (usually covered if sudden) and the plumbing repair (usually not, treated as a maintenance item). A slow, long-term seepage leak that caused gradual damage over months is far more likely to be denied than a leak that clearly started suddenly. Document the date you discovered it, the water bill spike, and photos before any demolition starts. For the Texas-specific version of this, see the [Texas Department of Insurance's guidance on water damage and mold coverage](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/when-are-water-damage-and-mold-covered-by-insurance.html) and the fuller walkthrough in [Water Damage Insurance Claim: How to File One in Texas Without Sinking It](/blog/water-damage-insurance-claim/).

Drying and documentation generally follow the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for water damage restoration, which is also the standard most adjusters expect a restoration invoice to reference.
