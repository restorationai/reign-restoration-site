If you're staring at a dark stain spreading across a bathroom ceiling, a musty smell that won't quit after a slow roof leak, or a remodel that just uncovered black growth behind a vanity, the first decision isn't which spray to buy. It's whether this is a wipe-it-yourself job or one that needs a licensed crew with containment and air scrubbing. Here's how to tell, and what actually happens once you make the call.

## Do you need a mold remediation company, or can you clean it yourself?
Call a licensed remediation company when visible mold covers more than roughly 10 square feet (about a 3-foot by 3-foot patch), when it followed a water event like a slow leak or flooding, or when it's hiding inside ductwork, insulation, or a wall cavity you can't fully see. The [EPA's mold cleanup guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home) treats anything smaller than that as a reasonable DIY scrub job with detergent and full drying.

Texas adds a specific trigger on top of that. Under the state's mold rules, any job larger than 25 contiguous square feet requires a written remediation protocol, and in many cases a separate licensed mold assessment consultant from the remediation contractor doing the work. You can confirm license status for any contractor through the [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation's license search](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/LicenseSearch/) before you let anyone start. Mold under a kitchen sink from a cabinet leak you caught quickly and dried out? Scrub it, dry it, move on. A ceiling stain after weeks of a hidden roof leak, or mold found after a North Texas humidity spike during a closed-up summer? That's a call.

## Who do you call first, the restoration company, a plumber, or your insurance?
Call the mold remediation company first if the mold has already formed. Call a plumber first only if water is still actively leaking and the source hasn't been stopped yet, since remediation can't really start until the water is off. Call your insurer after mitigation is underway, not before.

If a slab leak or a slow supply line is still feeding the problem, a licensed plumber needs to cut the water first. Once the source is stopped, a remediation crew can assess, contain, and remove the growth. Insurance comes in during or just after that step, because most homeowners policies only cover mold when it stems from a sudden, covered water loss, not from gradual seepage or long-term humidity you didn't address. Coverage details vary by carrier and policy language, so check yours before assuming either way, and keep every photo and receipt from the moment you notice the problem.

## What happens when you actually call?
The first call is a short phone triage: where the mold is, roughly how big the area is, whether there's been a recent leak, and whether anyone in the home has respiratory sensitivities. From there, a licensed technician schedules an on-site inspection with moisture meters to map how far the damage spreads behind the visible surface.

Have this ready before you call: photos of the affected area, the approximate timeline (when you first noticed it, whether there was a water event), and access info if the property is vacant. On arrival, the crew sets up containment, poly sheeting sealed across doorways and surrounding walls, following the [ANSI/IICRC S520 mold remediation standard](https://iicrc.org/s520/), runs HEPA air scrubbers, and works the exposed, affected surface directly with HEPA vacuums and antimicrobial treatment before giving you a written scope. If you want to understand testing options first, our post on [how to test for mold](/blog/how-to-test-for-mold/) walks through DIY kits versus a professional air sample.
