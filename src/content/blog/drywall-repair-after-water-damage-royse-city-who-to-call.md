## Who should I call first when drywall gets wet?

Call a water damage restoration company before you call anyone who repairs or paints drywall. A restoration crew stops further damage, pulls moisture readings, and decides what can be dried versus what has to be cut out, all of which has to happen before a rebuild starts.

If the water source is still active (a supply line, a water heater, a slow roof leak during rain), the very first call is whatever stops the water: a plumber for a plumbing failure, or the main shutoff if you can reach it yourself. Once the water is off, the next call is the restoration side of the job, not the repair side.

Homeowners in Royse City often default to calling a handyman or painter because the wall looks like a cosmetic problem: a stain, a bubble, a soft spot. It isn't, until it's been measured and dried.

## Why doesn't a painter or handyman come first?

A painter patches what's in front of them; they don't measure what's still wet behind the drywall, and patching over damp material traps moisture that can turn into mold within 24 to 48 hours, per the [EPA's guidance on mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home).

Drywall is porous. Paint and texture are not. A patch skims the surface, but the paper backing, the insulation behind it, and the base plate below it can stay wet for days if nobody checks them with a moisture meter. Drying follows the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), which calls for documented moisture readings, not a guess based on how the wall looks or feels.

This is the handoff gap: a painter who patches first, then a restoration company who has to tear that same patch back out two weeks later when mold shows up, costs more than doing it in the right order once.

## Can wet drywall be saved, or does it need to be cut out?

Wet drywall can sometimes be dried and saved if the water was clean, the wall was dry within 24 to 48 hours, and the drywall hasn't swollen or lost its structural integrity. Beyond that window, or with contaminated water, it comes out.

A few things decide it:

- **Water category.** Clean water from a supply line (Category 1) gives drywall the best odds of drying in place. Water from a sewer backup or storm flooding (Category 3, also called black water) means the drywall and any wet insulation are removed, not dried, because the material can't be made sanitary.
- **How long it sat.** Drywall that's been wet more than 48 hours is treated as a mold risk even if it looks fine, per EPA guidance.
- **Physical condition.** Sagging, crumbling, or a paper layer that's separated from the gypsum core means the panel has already lost its strength and has to be replaced regardless of category.
- **How high the water climbed.** Only the wet section typically comes out. A restoration tech marks the waterline and cuts a clean, level line above it rather than removing a whole wall unnecessarily.

If the trim at the bottom of the wall is swollen or the paint has lifted, that's often the first visible sign something's wrong behind it, the same read our [baseboard water damage guide](/blog/water-damage-baseboard/) walks through in more detail.

## How do you know if drywall has water damage, and could there be mold behind it?

Look for staining, a chalky or soft texture when you press it, bubbling paint, a musty smell, or tape lines at the seams that have started to separate. Any one of those means the drywall has taken on moisture, even without a visible wet spot.

Mold doesn't need a flood to get started. A slow leak behind a wall can feed mold growth for weeks before anyone notices a stain, and the smell often shows up before the visible damage does. If a wall has been damp for an extended period, or a previous leak was patched without drying the cavity first, it's worth having it checked before a rebuild closes the wall back up. ## Does insurance pay for drywall replacement after water damage?

Most standard homeowners policies cover drywall replacement when the water loss was sudden and accidental, like a burst supply line or a failed appliance hose, but not when it came from a long-term leak or poor maintenance. Check your policy; this varies by insurer.

A gradual leak behind a wall, the kind that caused the staining in the first place, is the scenario insurers scrutinize most closely, since policies generally exclude damage from ongoing seepage rather than a sudden event. Documentation protects the claim either way: dated photos, moisture readings, and a written scope of the drywall that's being removed versus dried. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) has a plain-language overview of what standard policies typically include.
